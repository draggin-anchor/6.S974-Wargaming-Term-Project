\section{Setting and research questions}
%======================================================================
\textbf{Contested system:} China and the United States compete for day-to-day control of the waters inside the
First Island Chain (Senkakus, Taiwan Strait, Luzon Strait, Scarborough and Second Thomas Shoals, Paracels)
\emph{below the threshold of war}. China claims most of the South China Sea inside its nine-dash line, contests
the Senkakus, and presses these claims through persistent presence with coast guard and maritime militia
(CCG) hulls backed by stronger, more heavily equipped navy (PLAN) ships. The U.S.\ rejects maritime claims beyond
what international law allows, including territorial seas around artificial islands, and asserts freedom of the
high seas, open shipping lanes, and area access through a smaller number of destroyers (DDG) and littoral combat
ships (LCS) on presence and freedom-of-navigation patrols \citep{helmus2024,green2017}. Each side decides, every two days,
\emph{where} to station its ships on a network of zones and \emph{how} to behave there: patrol (P) or
assert (A: shadowing; close maneuvering; querying and warning; and, for the U.S.\ only, freedom-of-navigation
operations).

\textbf{Why strategic interaction is central:}
Control of a zone is a function of the ratio between local forces. Therefore, each actor's best allocation depends on the other's allocation. Escalation risk is shared between both parties, where incidents need both sides present and escalation to war costs both sides. Neither side sees the other's entire fleet disposition: each reliably sees only the forces present where it also maintains forces.

\textbf{Research questions:}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
\item \emph{Control vs.\ escalation.} Each side's allocation is a MIP with an escalation-risk budget
$\varepsilon_i$. How much control does a larger $\varepsilon_i$ buy, at what war risk, and which budgets are
equilibrium choices when both sides pick them?
\item \emph{Strategic vs.\ heuristic play.} Do game-theoretic policies (equilibrium and maximin strategies,
exact best responses) beat random and rule-based ones, and can mutual restraint be sustained in repeated play?
\item \emph{Robustness and redesign.} Is the best policy or Blue design under nominal conditions also the most
robust to weather (Nature's scenario $\omega$), in expected and worst-case terms?
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Outcomes (averaged over 200 seeds):} Blue's value-weighted control share
$\bar c_\B=\frac1\tau\sum_t\sum_{v}V^\B_v c^t_v/\sum_v V^\B_v\in[-1,1]$, war rate, payoffs $u_\B,u_\Rd$, and for RQ3
the expected and worst-case payoff over $\omega$.

\section{Literature and current practice}
%======================================================================
\textbf{Documented practice:} 
\citet{helmus2024} describe China’s use of coast guard and maritime militia vessels to 
maintain presence and obstruct maritime activities while seeking to avoid a conventional military response. 
\citet{green2017} emphasize credible commitments, coordinated allied responses, and acceptance of calculated escalation risk while exercising restraint.
U.S.\ practice is defined by routine presence and freedom-of-navigation patrols, with encounters managed
by the Code for Unplanned Encounters at Sea \citep{cues2014}. From these sources, we define the action set (presence,
patrol, and assert), the asymmetric hull types, and the idea that incidents drive escalation, not shots.

\textbf{Models:} 
\citet{wasser2019} developed a structured, gray-zone game with explicit objectives and probabilistic adjudication; our model builds on that idea by using 
fully automated rules to support repeated simulation and strategy comparison. \citet{shieh2012} document a deployed Stackelberg model for randomized Coast Guard port patrols. 
\citep{washburn1995} model maritime interdiction and routing as zero-sum games. \citep{schelling1966} represents escalation as a shared,
manipulated risk, which motivated our explicit incident hazard.

\textbf{What we add:} We combine maritime allocation, evolving control, and shared escalation risk in a general sum game where both sides contest control simultaneously and war costs both. Computational rules support comparisons of strategies and designs across thousands of runs. Numerical parameters are our
assumptions (Table~\ref{tab:params}), not values derived from documented literature.

\section{Game formulation}

This section defines the game in the order a reader needs it: who plays and on what map (\S\ref{sec:setup}),
what describes the situation and what each side can see (\S\ref{sec:state}), what each side decides
(\S\ref{sec:actions}), how the situation then changes (\S\ref{sec:dynamics}), how each side scores
(\S\ref{sec:payoffs}), and how a side chooses its moves (\S\ref{sec:mip}--\ref{sec:policies}).
Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the main symbols with their meanings and values, and Table~\ref{tab:zones} the zone
values and weather scenarios. All numbers are our assumptions; there is no public data for them.

\subsection{Players, map and time}\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Players:} $\I=\{\B,\Rd\}$. Blue is the U.S.\ Navy. Red is China, with navy (PLAN) ships and coast guard
or maritime militia hulls, grouped as CCG. Ship types are $\K_\B=\{\text{DDG}\}$ and
$\K_\Rd=\{\text{PLAN},\text{CCG}\}$, with fleets of 10 DDG, 8 PLAN and 6 CCG.
% TODO (Sean): LCS are named in Section 1 but are not in the model yet. Add them as a second Blue type

\textbf{Map:} the theater is an undirected graph $G=(\V,\E)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:net}, Lecture 2). Its nodes are home
ports, where ships refuel, and eight contested zones
$\C=\{\text{SEN},\text{MIY},\text{TWN},\text{EOT},\text{LUZ},\text{SCB},\text{STS},\text{PAR}\}$ (Senkakus, Miyako
Strait, Taiwan Strait, east of Taiwan, Luzon Strait, Scarborough Shoal, Second Thomas Shoal, Paracels). Blue's
home ports are $H_\B=\{\text{YOK},\text{GUA}\}$ (Yokosuka, Guam) and Red's are $H_\Rd=\{\text{NBO},\text{SAN}\}$
(Ningbo, Sanya); neither side may enter the other's ports. An edge joins two nodes that a ship can sail between in
one period. The zones form three regions that share weather: North $=\{\text{SEN},\text{MIY}\}$, Center
$=\{\text{TWN},\text{EOT},\text{LUZ}\}$ and South $=\{\text{SCB},\text{STS},\text{PAR}\}$. Zone $v$ is worth $V^i_v$
to player $i$ (Table~\ref{tab:zones}); the two sides value zones differently (Scarborough Shoal and the Paracels
matter more to Red).

\textbf{Time:} the game is played in periods $t=1,\dots,T$ of two days each, with $T=60$ (about four months).
Payoffs in later periods are discounted by $\delta=0.98$ per period (Lectures 5--6). All ships start in port with
full endurance: Blue's DDGs are split 5/5 between YOK and GUA, and each Red type is split evenly between NBO and SAN.

\subsection{State, information and weather}\label{sec:state}
\textbf{State:} the situation at the start of period $t$ is $s^t=(x^t,d^t,c^t,\ell^t,w^t)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $x^t_{k,v}\in\Z_{\ge0}$: the number of type-$k$ ships at node $v$.
\item $d^t$: each ship's \emph{endurance}, the number of periods it can still stay at sea (at most $D_k$: 10 for a
DDG, 8 for a PLAN ship, 6 for a CCG hull). It drops by one each period at sea and refills after a full period in port.
\item $c^t_v\in[-1,1]$: the \emph{control index} of zone $v$. $+1$ means Blue is fully in charge there, $-1$ means
Red is, and $0$ means neither is. It changes gradually as presence builds or fades (\S\ref{sec:dynamics}).
\item $\ell^t\in\{0,1,\dots,L\}$: the \emph{escalation level}, a ladder of tension from calm ($0$) to armed
conflict ($L=5$), which ends the game. The game starts at $\ell^1=1$.
\item $w^t_r\in\{\text{calm},\text{rough},\text{storm}\}$: the sea state in region $r$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Information:} each player sees its own ships and the public quantities $c^t$, $\ell^t$ and $w^t$, but not
where the opponent's ships are now. Each period it detects the opponent's ships at node $v$ with probability
\[
q_{i,v}=\begin{cases}
0.95 & \text{if $i$ has a ship at $v$,}\\
0.6 & \text{if $i$ has a ship at a neighboring node, or $v$ borders one of $i$'s home ports,}\\
0.2 & \text{otherwise (satellite),}
\end{cases}
\]
multiplied by $0.7$ in rough or stormy seas. A detection shows the opponent's ship count of each type at $v$ and its
posture there. Player $i$'s \emph{belief} $\hat x^t_{-i,v}$ is the last count it detected at $v$ (exact at the
start, when every ship is in port). So both sides choose their moves at information sets that do not reveal where
the opponent is now (Lectures 5--8).

\textbf{Weather (Nature's move):} the weather is a random factor that neither side controls. We study four weather
scenarios $\omega\in\Omega=\{$calm, nominal, typhoon, extreme$\}$ with weights
$\pi_\omega=(0.25,\allowbreak0.40,\allowbreak0.25,\allowbreak0.10)$ (Lecture 1). In scenario $\omega$, each period
and in each region not already in a storm, a storm starts with probability $p_s(\omega)$; otherwise the sea is rough
with probability $p_r(\omega)$ and calm if not (Table~\ref{tab:zones}). A storm lasts three periods. It closes the
region's zones (no ship may enter, and ships inside stay put), stops incidents there, and freezes their control.
Rough seas halve the strength of CCG hulls (\S\ref{sec:dynamics}) and degrade detection. \S4.5 scores each policy
and design by its expected payoff over $\omega$ and by its worst case (Lecture 1).

\subsection{Actions and constraints}\label{sec:actions}
\textbf{Actions:} both players choose at the same time. Each period player $i$ chooses
\begin{itemize}
\item a destination for each of its ships: stay, or sail one edge to a neighboring node; and
\item a posture $p_{i,v}\in\{\Pt,\As\}$ in each zone $v$ where it will have ships. \emph{Patrol} (P) is routine
presence. \emph{Assert} (A) stands for the actions listed in \S1 (shadowing, close maneuvering, querying and
warning, and Blue's freedom-of-navigation operations), treated as one posture. Asserting makes the side's ships in
that zone count 1.5 times as much for control, but makes incidents more likely (\S\ref{sec:dynamics}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Constraints:} write $f^t_{k,uv}$ for the number of type-$k$ ships that sail from $u$ to $v$ in period $t$,
and $\mathcal N(u)$ for the neighbors of node $u$. Then
\begin{align*}
&x^{t+1}_{k,u}=x^t_{k,u}+\textstyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal N(u)}f^t_{k,vu}-\sum_{v\in\mathcal N(u)}f^t_{k,uv}
&&\text{(flow conservation: ships are moved, never created; Lecture 2)}\\
&\textstyle\sum_{k\in\K_i}\sum_{(u,v)}f^t_{k,uv}\le F_i
&&\text{(fuel: at most $F_\B=8$ and $F_\Rd=10$ ship moves per period)}
\end{align*}
and three more rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Endurance.} A ship may not sail to a node from which it could not get home on its remaining endurance,
unless the move brings it closer to home. A ship whose endurance has fallen to its distance from home is on
\emph{forced return}: it sails one edge toward port each period, then spends one full period in port to refit,
after which $d$ resets to $D_k$. Forced moves use fuel first; the player decides only for its other (\emph{free})
ships.
\item \emph{Ports and storms.} No ship may enter the opponent's home ports or a storm-closed zone.
\item \emph{Infeasible orders.} A planned move that breaks a rule, or that exceeds the fuel left, is not carried
out: the ship stays where it is.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How the state changes: control, incidents and escalation}\label{sec:dynamics}
After the moves, steps 1--3 happen in every contested zone $v$ that is not in a storm, and step 4 updates the
escalation level once for the whole theater.

\textbf{1. Local strength:} each side's weight of presence in the zone is
\[
S_{i,v}=\alpha^{\1[p_{i,v}=\As]}\textstyle\sum_{k\in\K_i}w_k\,x_{k,v},
\]
a weighted count of its hulls there. A DDG or a PLAN ship counts $w_k=1$; a CCG hull counts $0.6$ (it is smaller and
lightly armed), and only $0.3$ in rough seas. Asserting multiplies the side's strength by $\alpha=1.5$.

\textbf{2. Control:} the \emph{local balance}
$\phi_v=(S_{\B,v}-S_{\Rd,v})/(S_{\B,v}+S_{\Rd,v}+\kappa)$ lies between $-1$ and $1$ and is positive when Blue is
locally stronger. The constant $\kappa=1$ keeps a small presence from counting as full control: one DDG alone gives
$\phi_v=0.5$. Each period, control moves a fraction $\lambda=0.3$ of the way toward the local balance:
\begin{equation}
c^{t+1}_v=(1-\lambda)\,c^t_v+\lambda\,\phi_v .\label{eq:control}
\end{equation}
So control is earned by staying present for several periods and fades when a side leaves. In a storm, $c_v$ does
not change.

\textbf{3. Incidents:} an incident (a collision, an unsafe intercept, a standoff that goes wrong) can happen only
where both sides have ships. It occurs with probability $\rho_v=1-e^{-h_v}$, where the \emph{hazard} $h_v$ is
\begin{equation}
h_v=\Big(h_0+h^\B_\As\,\1[p_{\B,v}=\As]+h^\Rd_{\As,v}\,\1[p_{\Rd,v}=\As]\Big)\cdot\min\{S_{\B,v},S_{\Rd,v}\}
\cdot\big(1+\ell^t/2\big).\label{eq:hazard}
\end{equation}
The three factors read as follows. (i) Routine encounters carry a small baseline hazard $h_0=0.006$, and each side
that asserts adds to it: $h^\B_\As=0.03$ for Blue; for Red, $h^\Rd_{\As,v}=0.036\,\theta_v+0.012\,(1-\theta_v)$,
where $\theta_v$ is the PLAN share of Red's strength at $v$, so pressure from coast guard hulls is less escalatory
than pressure from navy ships. (ii) More ships in contact mean more encounters, and the smaller of the two forces
limits how many there can be. (iii) Incidents are more likely when tension is already high: at $\ell=3$ the hazard
is $2.5$ times its value at $\ell=0$.

\textbf{4. Escalation:} each incident raises $\ell$ by one. A period with no incident anywhere lowers $\ell$ by one
with probability $\mu=0.25$. If $\ell$ reaches $L=5$, the crisis has become an armed conflict and the game ends.

\subsection{Payoffs}\label{sec:payoffs}
\textbf{Stage payoff:} at the end of period $t$, player $i$ receives
\begin{equation}
g_i^t=\underbrace{\sigma_i\textstyle\sum_{v\in\C}V^i_v\,c^{t+1}_v}_{\text{control}}
\;-\;\underbrace{\gamma\,m^t_i}_{\text{fuel}}
\;-\;\underbrace{\eta\,\ell^{t+1}}_{\text{tension}}
\;-\;\underbrace{\zeta_D\,\1[i=\B]}_{\text{redesign cost}}.\label{eq:stage}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Control.} $\sigma_\B=+1$ and $\sigma_\Rd=-1$ turn the shared control index into each side's gain: Blue
gains $V^\B_v$ for each unit of $c_v$ above zero, and Red gains $V^\Rd_v$ for each unit below zero.
\item \emph{Fuel.} $m^t_i$ is the number of ship moves player $i$ made in period $t$, at $\gamma=0.1$ each.
\item \emph{Tension.} $\eta=1$ per escalation level per period stands for the diplomatic and economic cost of a
crisis.
\item \emph{Redesign cost.} $\zeta_D$ is the running cost of Blue's redesign $D$ (\S\ref{sec:designs}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Total payoff:} the game ends at $\tau$, the first period with $\ell^\tau=L$ (war), or at $T$. Player $i$'s
payoff is the discounted sum of its stage payoffs, minus a war penalty $W=200$ if the game ends in war:
\begin{equation}
u_i=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\delta^{t-1}g_i^t-W\,\1[\ell^\tau=L].\label{eq:payoff}
\end{equation}
Both sides pay $W$, so the game is general-sum, not zero-sum (Lecture 3): both prefer to avoid war. For scale,
Blue earns at most $\sum_vV^\B_v=42$ in a period in which it fully controls every zone, so a war costs about five
such periods.

\subsection{How a player decides: the best-response MIP with a risk budget}\label{sec:mip}
Our main decision rule, \emph{MIP-BR}, chooses each period the moves and postures that score best given what the
player believes, while capping its risk of incidents. It is a best response (Lectures 1 and 3) to the opponent's
believed positions, written as a mixed-integer program (Lecture 2) and solved with Gurobi, as in the lecture code.

\textbf{What the player takes as given:} its beliefs $\hat x_{-i}$, the opponent postures it last saw (patrol if
never seen), the sea state, the escalation level $\ell^t$, and its own ships. Ships on forced return are fixed;
$\bar x_{k,v}$ counts them by where they will be.

\textbf{Decision variables:} $y_{s,w}\in\{0,1\}$ equals 1 if free ship $s$ goes to node $w$ (it may stay, sail
home, or sail to a neighbor from which it can still get home). Control and incident risk are nonlinear in the ship
counts (a ratio and an exponential), so we list every count a zone could end up with: $b_{v,(n,p)}\in\{0,1\}$ equals
1 if the player will have $n=(n_k)_{k\in\K_i}$ hulls in zone $v$ with posture $p$. Exactly one option is chosen per
zone, and each option's value and risk are computed in advance, which keeps the program linear.

\textbf{Value of an option:}
\[
a_v(n,p)=V^i_v\,\beta\,\sigma_i\phi_v(n,p)-\Lambda\,\rho_v(n,p),\qquad\beta=\frac{\lambda}{1-\delta(1-\lambda)}\approx0.96 .
\]
Here $\phi_v(n,p)$ and $\rho_v(n,p)$ are the local balance and the incident probability of \S\ref{sec:dynamics} if
the player brings $n$ hulls with posture $p$ and the opponent is where the player believes it is. The factor
$\beta$ turns one period of local balance into its total discounted effect on control: by \eqref{eq:control}, a
balance $\phi_v$ moves $c_v$ by $\lambda\phi_v$ now, a fraction $1-\lambda$ of that change survives each later
period, and the discounted sum is $\lambda\sum_{j\ge0}(\delta(1-\lambda))^j=\beta$. $\Lambda=20$ is the price the
player puts on one expected incident, since an incident raises $\ell$ (costing $\eta$ every period) and brings war
closer.

\textbf{The program:}
\begin{align*}
\max_{y,\,b}\ \ &\textstyle\sum_{v\in\C}\sum_{(n,p)}a_v(n,p)\,b_{v,(n,p)}
-\gamma\sum_{s}\sum_{w\ne\text{loc}(s)}y_{s,w}
&&\text{(control value $-$ incident cost $-$ fuel cost)}\\
\text{s.t.}\ \ &\textstyle\sum_w y_{s,w}=1\quad\forall s
&&\text{(each free ship gets one destination)}\\
&\textstyle\sum_{s}\sum_{w\ne\text{loc}(s)}y_{s,w}\le F_i-\text{(forced moves)}
&&\text{(fuel)}\\
&\textstyle\sum_{(n,p)}b_{v,(n,p)}=1\quad\forall v
&&\text{(one option per zone)}\\
&\textstyle\sum_{(n,p)}n_k\,b_{v,(n,p)}=\bar x_{k,v}+\sum_{s\text{ of type }k}y_{s,v}\quad\forall v,k
&&\text{(the option matches the moves)}\\
&\textstyle\sum_{v\in\C}\sum_{(n,p)}\rho_v(n,p)\,b_{v,(n,p)}\le\varepsilon_i\,(1-\ell^t/L)
&&\text{(escalation-risk budget)}\\
&y,\,b\ \text{binary.}
\end{align*}
\textbf{The escalation-risk budget:} the left side of the last constraint is the player's expected number of
incidents this period. It accepts at most $\varepsilon_i$ of them, and the cap shrinks to zero as $\ell$ approaches
war. The budget defines the feasible region, the set of allocations the player considers safe enough. The
proposal's conservative, moderate and aggressive commanders become budgets $\varepsilon_i\in\{0.05,0.15,0.40\}$,
and RQ1 varies $\varepsilon_i$. If forced returns alone break the budget, a slack variable with a large penalty
keeps the program feasible. The program looks only one period ahead; $\beta$ is how it values the future effect of
control.

\subsection{The template game and the policies we compare}\label{sec:policies}
\textbf{Template game (Lectures 3--4):} the full game has far too many strategies to solve for an equilibrium
directly, so we also study a small strategic-form version. Each player picks one of 14 \emph{templates}: one of
seven ways to split its deployable ships across North/Center/South (60/20/20, 20/60/20, 20/20/60, 40/40/20,
40/20/40, 20/40/40 or 34/33/33 percent), combined with one posture (P or A) in all its zones. Deployable means 70\%
of each fleet, spread evenly over the zones of a region (Red uses PLAN and CCG in proportion; fractional hulls are
allowed). If both sides kept their templates, control would settle at $c_v=\phi_v$ (set $c^{t+1}_v=c^t_v$ in
\eqref{eq:control}). So the payoff of a template pair $(a_\B,a_\Rd)$ is that settled control minus the incident
cost, in calm seas at $\ell=1$:
\[
M_i(a_\B,a_\Rd)=\sigma_i\textstyle\sum_{v\in\C}V^i_v\,\phi_v-\Lambda\sum_{v\in\C}\rho_v .
\]
This gives a finite game $\Gamma=\langle\I,(S_i),(M_i)\rangle$ with $|S_i|=14$, which we analyze with iterated
elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS), enumeration of pure Nash equilibria, the maximin LP and the
correlated-equilibrium LP.

\textbf{Policies:} in the simulations (\S4) each side follows one of these rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{random}: random legal moves and postures (a benchmark).
\item \emph{rule-based} (``moderate''): sends ships to the zones with the best value per unit of opponent strength;
asserts only where it believes it outnumbers the opponent and $\ell\le2$; then drops assertions and pulls ships back
until its expected incidents fit the risk budget $\varepsilon=0.15\,(1-\ell/L)$.
\item \emph{greedy}: a fast heuristic for the MIP of \S\ref{sec:mip} (same objective and budget, no solver).
\item \emph{MIP-BR}$(\varepsilon)$: solves the MIP exactly each period.
\item \emph{equilibrium}: plays its part of a pure Nash equilibrium of $\Gamma$, steering its free ships toward the
template's split (more ships to the more valuable zones of a region). $\Gamma$ has two pure equilibria; we use the
one with the higher total payoff, which is an assumption.
\item \emph{maximin}: every three periods, draws a template from its maximin mixed strategy of $\Gamma$, the one
that guarantees the best payoff whatever template the opponent plays.
\end{itemize}
The two template policies switch to patrol when $\ell>3$ and drop assertions that break their risk budget.

\subsection{Resilient redesigns (Blue)}\label{sec:designs}
A redesign changes Blue's system, not the way Blue plays it. In the terms of Lecture 1, D1 is a structural change
(an alternative path) and D2 and D3 are operational ones (monitoring and rapid response). Each is compared with the
baseline D0 in every weather scenario:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{D1 dispersed basing:} a third Blue port at Subic Bay (EDCA access), linked to SCB, LUZ and STS, which
gives alternative paths and shorter trips to the South. Blue starts with 4/3/3 DDGs at YOK/GUA/Subic.
Cost $\zeta_{\text{D1}}=0.3$ per period.
\item \textbf{D2 redundant sensing:} Blue detects in every zone with probability at least $0.6$, also in bad
weather. Cost $\zeta_{\text{D2}}=0.5$ per period.
\item \textbf{D3 crisis hotline} (CUES-style communication): once $\ell\ge3$, a quiet period lowers $\ell$ with
probability $\mu=0.5$ instead of $0.25$, and assert hazards are halved. We charge no cost yet ($\zeta_{\text{D3}}=0$),
and it helps Red as much as Blue.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Simplifying assumptions:} two unitary players; control is a force-ratio index; incidents are the only path
to war; beliefs are last-seen counts; best responses look one period ahead.
