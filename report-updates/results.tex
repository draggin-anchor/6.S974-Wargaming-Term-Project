\subsection{Template game and a small example: patrol or assert, and sustainable restraint}
IESDS reduces $\Gamma$ from $14\times14$ to $3\times2$ \wone{$2\times4$}. Blue keeps the center/south split (20/40/40) with
assert and the even split (34/33/33) with either posture. Red keeps only the center/south split, with either posture.
There are two pure NE. The \emph{equilibrium} policy plays (Blue even split patrol, Red center/south assert),
$(-22.96,\,16.60)$. The other is (Blue center/south assert, Red center/south patrol), $(-11.52,\,1.99)$. The
welfare-maximizing correlated equilibrium puts all its weight on the first.

Fixing these splits (Blue even, Red center/south), each side only chooses P or A. Normalized so (P,P) $=(0,0)$, the
$2\times2$ sub-matrix of $\Gamma$ has (P,A) $=(-10.36,\,2.19)$, (A,P) $=(1.06,\,-11.22)$ and (A,A) $=(-11.33,\,-10.49)$
(Blue, Red). For Red, asserting pays whatever Blue does ($2.19>0$ and $-10.49>-11.22$), so assert is a dominant strategy.
Blue gains a little by asserting against a patroller ($G_\B=1.06$), but against an asserting Red it loses less by
patrolling ($-10.36$ against $-11.33$). The unique NE is (P,A): Red asserts and Blue patrols. In Workshop 1 this game
had two pure NE, (A,P) and (P,A), and a mixed NE. The change comes from the fleets. Red now has about $1.6$ times Blue's
total strength, so it keeps control of a zone even when both sides assert.

This also ends the Workshop 1 restraint argument. There, falling back to the one-shot equilibrium after any assertion
was a threat that made mutual patrol a subgame perfect equilibrium. Now Red earns $2.19$ in the one-shot NE and
$0$ under mutual patrol, so that threat costs Red nothing, and mutual patrol cannot be held this way.

zone Blue leaves.

\smallskip\textbf{Risk-budget game} (Table~\ref{fig:rq1})\textbf{:} the structure is the same as in Workshop 1.
Whatever the other side picks, ``aggressive'' pays each side the most, so (aggressive, aggressive) is the unique pure
NE. Both sides would do better if both chose moderate ($-430,\,385$ against $-475,\,86$). This is still a prisoner's
dilemma, but a much sharper one. Mutual aggression now ends in war in $94\%$ of one-year games \wone{$34\%$ of
four-month games}. The highest total payoff is again (Blue conservative, Red moderate).

\textbf{MIP-BR is now Blue's best policy against every Red policy} (Table~\ref{tab:policies}), and it has the best worst
case ($-430$). Greedy is second ($-501$). The gap between them is $71$, about seven standard errors \wone{a gap of $7$,
too small to separate}. With many ship types, greedy finds the MIP's plan in only $24\%$ of $4{,}537$ Blue decisions
\wone{$67\%$ of $1{,}524$}, and it breaks the risk budget in $11\%$ \wone{$14\%$}.
The \textbf{equilibrium} and \textbf{maximin} plans fall far behind ($-718$ and $-710$ against MIP-BR) \wone{equilibrium

adapt to where Red is or to which of their own ships are available, and they end with a control share of $-0.33$
against $-0.15$ for MIP-BR. They do keep the war rate lowest ($0.08$ against MIP-BR). MIP-BR against MIP-BR has the highest war rate among the strong
policies ($0.26$).
